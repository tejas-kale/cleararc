Lesson 2 of 4 · FX & Central Banks

Reserves, and what it means to intervene

Where a country's war chest of foreign currency comes from, who is actually allowed to spend it, and the asymmetry that decides whether a currency defence can succeed.

≈7 minutes · Builds on Lesson 1

Reserves are a portfolio, not a vault

Foreign-exchange reserves are foreign-currency assets held by a country's monetary authorities. Not banknotes in a basement — overwhelmingly foreign government bonds, above all US Treasury securities, plus deposits at other central banks. They earn interest. This detail seems like trivia and turns out to be the entire second half of the article, so file it now: reserves are an investment portfolio that happens to be denominated in someone else's money.

To intervene is simply to trade that portfolio against your own currency in the open market, with the aim of moving the price rather than making money. Two directions:

  • Selling your own currency (to weaken it): you create yen, buy dollars, and your reserves grow.
  • Buying your own currency (to strengthen it): you spend dollars from reserves to buy yen back, and your reserves shrink.

July 2026 was the second kind. The article puts the size at "more than $95bn", sold at around ¥160 to the dollar.

The asymmetry that decides currency wars

These two directions are not mirror images, and the difference is the most useful single idea in this lesson.

To weaken your currency you sell something you can conjure. The BoJ's own description of the operation says that for dollar-buying intervention "the yen funds to be sold are raised by issuing Financing Bills"¹ — short-term government IOUs. There is no natural ceiling. A determined country can push its own currency down more or less indefinitely; the constraints are political and inflationary, not mechanical.

This is why accusations of "currency manipulation" are always about the weakening direction. Nobody suspects a country of cheating by making its exports more expensive.

To strengthen your currency you must sell dollars, and you can only sell dollars you already own. The same BoJ page: for yen-buying intervention, "U.S. dollar funds held in the FEFSA are used." That is a finite stock. You can run out.

Which is precisely the story the article opens with. In 1992 the Bank of England was buying pounds to defend sterling's peg, and George Soros's fund was selling pounds to it. The Bank had a finite pile of reserves; Soros could keep selling. The Bank ran out first and abandoned the defence — and Soros made a reported $1bn. When you read that Scott Bessent "once worked for Soros Fund Management, the hedge fund famous for 'breaking' the Bank of England", the joke is that he has switched to the side that can run out of ammunition.

Check yourself

Why can a central bank push its own currency down for longer than it can prop it up?

Selling your own currency draws on an unlimited supply — you issue it. Buying it back draws on a finite stock of somebody else's money. That single asymmetry is why speculators attack pegs from the "force a devaluation" side and essentially never from the other, and why a defence of a weak currency has a clock running on it.

Whose money is it? (The bit the newspapers blur)

Every account of July 2026 says "the Bank of Japan bought yen". Strictly, that is not who decided and not whose money it was.

In Japan, the Minister of Finance holds authority over foreign-exchange policy and decides whether to intervene; the Bank of Japan then executes the instruction as the minister's agent, using the Foreign Exchange Fund Special Account (FEFSA) — a government account, not the central bank's own book.² The article is careful about this where most coverage is not: "the BoJ, acting on behalf of Japan's finance ministry".

The same split exists in America. The New York Fed executes FX operations for the Fed's own account at the FOMC's direction, and for the Treasury's Exchange Stabilization Fund at the Treasury Secretary's direction.³ That second channel is what let Bessent act quickly and visibly — it is the Treasury's to deploy.

Why insist on the distinction? Because Lesson 3 is going to net the treasury and the central bank together into one balance sheet, and you cannot appreciate what that netting does unless you first see that they are genuinely separate institutions with separate books.

The trade that made ¥5.7 trillion

Now the payoff of "reserves are a portfolio". Japan accumulated much of this dollar pile in earlier decades, when it was intervening the other way to hold a too-strong yen down — and, as the article notes, "a big chunk of its reserves were accumulated when a dollar could be had for less than ¥100."

So it bought dollars cheap and, in July 2026, sold them dear. The arithmetic is worth doing yourself, because it is the whole basis of the article's claim that Japan out-traded Soros:

Do the multiplication once by hand and the number stops being a factoid and becomes something you could have derived: 95 × 160 = 15,200; 95 × 100 = 9,500; the gap is 5,700 billion yen. This is what the article means by "back-of-the-envelope arithmetic". You now own that envelope.

A caution worth carrying

Whether intervention actually moves the exchange rate for long is genuinely disputed among economists — not a settled thing that this lesson is simplifying. A Federal Reserve Board study of Japanese intervention from 1991 to 2004 is the standard reference for how mixed the evidence is.⁴ Notice that the article never claims the yen was rescued; it claims the trade was profitable and the balance sheet improved. Those are different claims, and it is choosing the defensible one.

Retrieval practice

From memory: name the institution that decided on Japan's July intervention, the institution that executed it, and the account the dollars came out of. Then say in one sentence why Japan's dollars were sitting on a large profit.

The Ministry of Finance decided (the Minister of Finance holds FX authority); the Bank of Japan executed it as the ministry's agent; the dollars came from the Foreign Exchange Fund Special Account, a government account. They were sitting on a profit because they were bought during earlier decades of yen strength, many when a dollar cost under ¥100, and were sold at about ¥160 — the currency move alone roughly 60% in Japan's favour.

What you can now read

The first four paragraphs of the article are now fully open to you: who Bessent is, what he "dipped into", why teaming up with Japan meant teaming up with a giant rather than rescuing a naïf, and where a ¥5.7trn profit comes from.

What remains mysterious is the article's real argument — that buying yen "unwinds a dangerous trade" and prunes the government's liabilities. To see that, you need the other side of the balance sheet. That is Lesson 3: bonds, the central bank, and one balance sheet.

Ask your teacher. A good stress-test question to bring back: if intervention's effect on the exchange rate is so doubtful, why do finance ministries keep doing it? There is a real answer involving signalling, and it is more interesting than the mechanics.
