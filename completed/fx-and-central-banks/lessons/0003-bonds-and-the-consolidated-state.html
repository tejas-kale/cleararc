Lesson 3 of 4 · FX & Central Banks

Bonds, and the state as one balance sheet

The hardest idea in the arc, and the one that makes the article's argument visible: what happens when you stop treating the treasury and the central bank as two institutions.

≈8 minutes · Builds on Lesson 2

A bond, in one paragraph

A government bond is a dated IOU. The state takes ¥100 today and promises fixed payments on a fixed schedule. Because the payments are fixed but the bond trades, its price moves and its yield — the return you earn buying at today's price and holding to maturity — moves inversely. If prevailing interest rates rise, an old bond paying 0.5% is worth less; its price falls until its yield matches what new bonds offer. Price down, yield up. Always. They are two readings of one number, in the same way that ¥163 was two readings of one exchange rate.

The crucial consequence for this lesson: a bond already issued locks in its cost to the issuer until it matures. Rates can triple and the government still pays 0.5% on that bond. The pain arrives only when the bond matures and must be "rolled over" — reissued at today's rate. Remember the word rolled; the article uses it in its last technical sentence.

Two facts follow that most newspaper readers never quite join up. First, a bond is an asset to whoever holds it and a liability to whoever issued it — same object, opposite signs. Second, a government borrowing in its own currency is in a categorically different position from one borrowing in someone else's, because it can always produce the yen to pay. Default is not the risk. Something else is, and this lesson is building toward it.

The chain the article describes

The government sells bonds in its own currency, many of which have been bought by the central bank. The central bank in turn owes yen to the commercial banks which hold accounts with it.

Unpack that in three steps.

One. Japan's government issues JGBs — Japanese government bonds — to fund itself. Liability of the state.

Two. Across two decades of quantitative easing, the Bank of Japan bought those bonds in enormous quantity, until it held roughly half of all JGBs outstanding. It did not buy them with taxes. When a central bank buys a bond from a commercial bank, it pays by crediting that bank's account at the central bank — an account every commercial bank is required to hold. Those balances are called reserves, and confusingly they have nothing whatever to do with the foreign-exchange reserves of Lesson 2. Same English word, unrelated things.

Two meanings of "reserves", both central to this article: FX reserves are foreign assets a country owns. Bank reserves (in Japan, "current account balances") are yen a central bank owes to commercial banks. One is an asset, the other a liability. Keeping them apart is most of the battle.

Three. Those reserves are a liability of the central bank. They are payable on demand, and the central bank pays interest on them at whatever its policy rate happens to be. So they behave like overnight floating-rate debt. When the BoJ raises rates, the cost of this liability reprices essentially immediately.

Now net it out

Here is the move. The treasury and the central bank are legally distinct — Lesson 2 insisted on that — but they are both parts of the state. So ask: what does the consolidated public sector owe to, and own from, the outside world? Anything the two owe each other cancels.

That last sentence is the payload of this lesson. Read it twice. Quantitative easing did not reduce the state's debt; it changed the debt's maturity. A government that thought it had locked in thirty years of near-zero borrowing costs is, once you consolidate, funding itself overnight at whatever rate the central bank sets tomorrow.

Check yourself

On a consolidated view, what did two decades of the BoJ buying JGBs actually accomplish?

The size barely moved; the structure moved completely. Each JGB absorbed by the central bank was paid for with newly created reserves that the state now owes to commercial banks, on demand, at the policy rate. This is why an economy can spend twenty years apparently immune to its own debt and then become acutely rate-sensitive the moment the central bank starts tightening.

The other side of the ledger

So much for what the state owes. What does it own? The article cites work by YiLi Chien of the St Louis Fed, Harold Cole of Penn and Hanno Lustig of Stanford:

…the government, the BoJ and Japan's public pension funds together hold foreign assets worth more than 56% of GDP. Over the quarter-century to 2023 these assets earned average annual returns of 5.8% (only a fraction of which have been realised through trades).

Note the parenthesis, which is doing quiet work. Most of that 5.8% is unrealised — it exists as higher marks on assets still held, not as cash banked. July's ¥5.7trn was one of the rare moments when a slice of it was actually crystallised. And note who is included: the pension funds too, chiefly the GPIF, one of the largest pools of retirement money on earth, which holds a great deal of foreign stocks and bonds.

Whether pension funds belong inside "the state" is a real modelling choice, not an obvious fact. Chien, Cole and Lustig argue they do, since their obligations are ultimately public. If you excluded them the position would be smaller but the same shape. Good sceptical instinct to have when a striking number depends on where a boundary is drawn.

The shape you are looking at

Stand back from the consolidated box. Japan's state:

  • owes yen, short-term, at a near-zero but now-rising floating rate;
  • owns foreign assets, long-term, risky, unhedged, yielding considerably more.

Borrow cheap in one currency, hold higher-yielding assets in another, don't hedge the currency risk. If that arrangement sounds like something with a name — it has one, and the article's title is about what happens when it starts to unwind.

Retrieval practice

Explain to an imaginary friend, without using the word "consolidated", why the Bank of Japan owning half of Japan's government bonds does not mean half the debt has gone away. Three sentences.

The central bank didn't pay for those bonds with money it had; it paid by crediting the accounts commercial banks hold with it. Those account balances are themselves a debt — owed on demand, and paying whatever the policy rate is. So the state swapped one obligation for another: it no longer owes thirty-year money at a fixed low rate, it owes overnight money at tomorrow's rate, and the total is much the same.

What you can now read

The article's dense middle paragraph — government bonds, bought by the central bank, which in turn owes yen to commercial banks — should now read as a mechanism rather than a list. The sentence immediately after it names the shape. That name, the rate-rise problem it creates, and the sterilisation mechanics of the intervention itself are Lesson 4: the carry trade, sterilisation, and the article decoded.

Ask your teacher. If the consolidation step felt like sleight of hand, say so — the objection "but they really are separate institutions with separate mandates" is a serious one that economists argue about, and the answer sharpens the concept rather than defending it.
