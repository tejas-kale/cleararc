Rules that always hold

  1. Ask which currency is the ruler. "¥163 to the dollar" prices the dollar in yen. A bigger number means a weaker yen.
  2. Bond price and yield move in opposite directions. They are two readings of one number.
  3. A state can weaken its currency forever; it can strengthen it only until reserves run out. The clock only runs on one side. This is why speculators attack pegs from the devaluation side.
  4. An issued bond locks in its cost until maturity. Rate rises reach fixed-rate debt only when it is rolled over.
  5. Bank reserves are overnight floating-rate debt. Quantitative easing therefore shortens the state's effective maturity — it does not shrink its debt.
  6. A weak currency retires more domestic debt per foreign dollar spent. ¥160 of liabilities per dollar, versus ¥100 when the yen is strong.

The consolidation, in one move

Set the treasury and the central bank side by side and cancel what they owe each other:

  • The JGBs the central bank holds are the state's IOU to itself → cancel.
  • The bank reserves created to buy them are owed outside → survive.
  • Result: long fixed-rate debt has become overnight floating-rate debt.

Then read the surviving position: short yen, floating, near-zero · long foreign assets, unhedged, higher-yielding. That is a carry trade, whoever is running it.

Step 2 swaps which liability is owed; it does not undo step 1. Both sides of the carry trade shrink.

Sanity check on any big number

$95bn sounds enormous and retires under 1% of Japan's interest-bearing liabilities. Always divide the headline figure by the stock it is acting on before deciding how impressed to be.
