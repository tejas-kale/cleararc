Reference · FX & Central Banks

The article, decoded

"When Japan buys yen, it unwinds a dangerous trade" — The Economist, 13 August 2026 — walked through its load-bearing sentences, each with the machinery behind it.

Short quotations for study. Read the full piece at the source.

The opening: who is betting on what

"He once worked for Soros Fund Management, the hedge fund famous for 'breaking' the Bank of England during the sterling crisis of 1992. But in late July Mr Bessent bet the other way…"

The joke rests on the asymmetry. In 1992 the Bank of England was buying pounds with a finite stock of reserves while Soros sold pounds without limit; the Bank ran out first. Bessent has changed sides — he is now on the side with the ammunition clock.

"He dipped into America's foreign-exchange reserves to help the Bank of Japan (BoJ) buy yen…"

Specifically the Exchange Stabilization Fund, the Treasury's own FX pot, deployable at the Secretary's direction and executed by the New York Fed. This is why a Treasury Secretary rather than the Federal Reserve chair is the one making the trade — in America, currency policy is Treasury's, not the central bank's.

"…which had weakened past ¥163 to the dollar for the first time since 1986."

Yen per dollar, so a higher number is a weaker yen — see Lesson 1. Forty-year lows. For Japan that means expensive imported food and fuel, which is a household-level inflation problem and therefore a political one.

"…the BoJ, acting on behalf of Japan's finance ministry, may have spent more than $95bn…"

Note the precision most coverage skips. The Minister of Finance decides; the BoJ merely executes as agent, spending from the government's Foreign Exchange Fund Special Account. Not the central bank's money and not the central bank's call.

The profit

"It sold the dollars for about ¥160 apiece. A big chunk of its reserves were accumulated when a dollar could be had for less than ¥100."

Reserves are a portfolio with a cost basis, not a vault of cash. These dollars were bought during earlier decades when Japan was intervening the other way, selling yen to stop it strengthening. Bought near ¥100, sold at ¥160.

"Back-of-the-envelope arithmetic suggests it may have reaped as much as ¥5.7trn ($36bn)."

Do it yourself: 95 × 160 = ¥15.2trn received; 95 × 100 = ¥9.5trn paid; the gap is ¥5.7trn. "As much as" because not every dollar was bought at ¥100. For scale, Soros's celebrated raid on sterling made about $1bn, roughly $2.4bn in today's money.

The balance sheet

"…the government, the BoJ and Japan's public pension funds together hold foreign assets worth more than 56% of GDP. Over the quarter-century to 2023 these assets earned average annual returns of 5.8% (only a fraction of which have been realised through trades)."

From Chien, Cole and Lustig, What About Japan? Two things to notice. The three institutions are being lumped together deliberately — that is the consolidation. And the parenthesis concedes that most of the gain is paper, unrealised; July was one of the rare moments a slice became cash.

"The government sells bonds in its own currency, many of which have been bought by the central bank. The central bank in turn owes yen to the commercial banks which hold accounts with it."

The densest sentence in the piece, and the hinge of the whole argument. The chain: JGBs are a state liability → the BoJ bought them with newly created bank reserves → those reserves are owed on demand, at the policy rate. Consolidate and the JGBs the BoJ holds cancel, leaving overnight floating-rate debt where long fixed-rate debt used to be.

"The public sector's balance-sheet has thus benefited both from favourable currency movements and from the difference in yields between the foreign assets it owns and the yen liabilities it owes, known as the 'carry'."

Two distinct income sources, worth keeping apart. The currency gain is the ¥100 → ¥160 move. The carry is the ongoing yield gap — foreign assets paying more than yen liabilities cost. The first is lumpy and reverses; the second drips in steadily.

Why it stopped being safe

"For as long as Japan was fighting stagnant demand and deflationary pressure, its gigantic carry trade was sustainable."

The trade was underwritten by the problem that made it necessary. Deflation guaranteed a zero policy rate, and a zero policy rate is what made the funding free. Circular, and stable while the circle held.

"…it would quickly raise the borrowing costs for Japan's combined public sector by 0.75% of GDP… with more to come when longer-term liabilities are rolled over."

Both halves are explained by the consolidation. Quickly, because bank reserves are overnight and reprice at once. More to come, because the remaining fixed-rate bonds only feel the new rate as each one matures and is reissued.

The mechanism

"Any dollars the BoJ sells to commercial banks will drain their yen accounts at the central bank, reducing the amount the public sector owes to them."

Step one. Banks pay for the dollars out of their accounts at the BoJ, so bank reserves — a state liability — fall by ¥15.2trn.

"…the BoJ's interventions are typically 'sterilised': the central bank buys government securities from the banks (usually bills issued by the finance ministry) thus restoring their yen balances."

Step two, and it exists only to undo step one's side-effect. Draining reserves would tighten monetary policy by accident, possibly contradicting the interest rate the BoJ has publicly set. Sterilisation separates the currency lever from the interest-rate lever.

"But the net result is still that the exchequer, and therefore Japan's consolidated public sector, has fewer yen liabilities to worry about."

The easy misreading is that step two cancels step one. It does not — it swaps which liability is owed. Bank reserves come back, but an equal quantity of finance-ministry bills leaves private hands and returns to the state, where it cancels. Net across both steps: −$95bn of foreign assets, −¥15.2trn of yen liabilities. Both ends of the carry trade shrank.

"And because Japan's currency has yet to rally decisively, the BoJ can extinguish lots of yen liabilities for every dollar sold."

The most elegant line in the piece. At ¥160 each dollar retires ¥160 of debt; at ¥100 it would retire only ¥100. A weak yen makes your dollars efficient at killing yen debt — so a government intending to shrink this position anyway should do it while its own currency is cheap. The currency defence and the debt pruning are the same transaction.

The honest caveat

"If the purchases last month did amount to $95bn, they would have extinguished less than 1% of the public sector's interest-bearing liabilities."

The writer supplies the number that undercuts their own headline — a good sign. $95bn is a nibble. And note what the article never claims: that the intervention worked in the sense of turning the yen around. Whether intervention moves exchange rates for long is genuinely disputed. The claim made here is narrower and far more defensible: the trade was profitable and the balance sheet improved.

The argument in one sentence

Japan's state accidentally became the world's largest carry trader; rising Japanese interest rates make that position dangerous; and buying yen while the yen is cheap shrinks the position at the best possible exchange rate — so what looks like a currency defence is also a well-timed, if small, unwinding.
