Reference · FX & Central Banks

Glossary

The canonical vocabulary for this workspace. Where several words exist for one idea, one is chosen and the rest are listed as aliases to avoid.

Prints to two pages · Terms are promoted here once used correctly, not when first met

The price

Exchange rate
The price of one currency expressed in another. Always a ratio with a direction: "¥163 to the dollar" prices the dollar, in yen.Avoid: the value of the yen, currency level
Weak / strong currency
Weak means each unit buys less foreign currency. For a yen-per-dollar quote, a higher number means a weaker yen.Avoid: the yen is up/down — ambiguous without the quote convention
Funding currency
The cheap currency a carry trade borrows in. The yen's role for decades.Avoid: base currency
Uncovered interest parity (UIP)
The proposition that an interest-rate advantage in one currency should be exactly offset by that currency's expected depreciation. Fails persistently in the data; the failure is what a carry trade harvests.Avoid: interest rate parity — that is the covered, hedged version, which does hold

The reserves

Foreign-exchange reserves
Foreign-currency assets held by a country's monetary authorities — mostly foreign government bonds, not cash. An investment portfolio denominated in someone else's money.Avoid: war chest, currency stockpile
Intervention
Trading reserves against one's own currency in the open market with the aim of moving the price rather than earning a return.Avoid: propping up the currency — describes only one direction
The asymmetry
A state can weaken its currency without limit, because it issues what it sells; it can strengthen it only until reserves run out. The reason speculative attacks come from one side.Avoid: no standard term — this workspace uses "the asymmetry"
FEFSA
Japan's Foreign Exchange Fund Special Account. A government account holding the FX reserves; the Bank of Japan spends from it only as the finance ministry's agent.Avoid: the BoJ's reserves
Exchange Stabilization Fund (ESF)
The US Treasury's equivalent pot, deployable at the Treasury Secretary's direction and executed by the New York Fed. What Bessent reached into.

The balance sheet

Government bond
A dated IOU issued by the state. An asset to its holder, a liability to the issuer, with a cost fixed at issue until it matures.Avoid: gilt, JGB, Treasury — use the local name only when the country matters
Yield
The return earned buying a bond at today's price and holding it to maturity. Moves inversely to price — two readings of one number.Avoid: interest rate, when the bond is already issued
Bank reserves
Balances commercial banks hold in their accounts at the central bank. A central-bank liability, payable on demand and paying the policy rate — so, effectively, overnight floating-rate debt. In Japan, "current account balances".Avoid: reserves, unqualified — collides with FX reserves
Consolidated public sector
Treasury and central bank (and, for Japan, the public pension funds) viewed as one entity, with everything they owe each other netted out. The view that makes the carry position visible.Avoid: the government, when the central bank is included
Rolling over
Reissuing a maturing bond at today's interest rate. The channel through which a rate rise reaches fixed-rate debt, slowly.Avoid: refinancing

The trade

Carry trade
Borrowing in a low-yielding currency to hold higher-yielding assets in another, without hedging the currency risk.Avoid: currency arbitrage — arbitrage is riskless, and this is not
Carry
The interest-rate gap between what a position earns and what it costs to fund. The steady part of a carry trade's return; the currency move is the volatile part.Avoid: spread, yield pickup
Sterilised intervention
An intervention paired with an offsetting open-market operation, so the currency trade happens without changing the money supply or the policy rate.Avoid: neutralised, offset intervention
Unwinding
Closing a position — here, shrinking both sides of the state's carry trade at once by selling foreign assets and extinguishing yen liabilities.Avoid: deleveraging, when only one side is moving
Ambiguity resolved

In this workspace, unqualified "reserves" always means foreign-exchange reserves — a state's assets. The liability kind is always written in full as "bank reserves". The article uses both senses within four paragraphs of each other, which is the single most common place to lose the thread.
