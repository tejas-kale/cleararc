Lesson 3 of 5 · Paired email evaluation

Compare models email by email

Using the same emails for every model gives a cleaner comparison. The evidence is in their disagreements.

≈10 minutes · Paired differences and uncertainty

Define the paired counts

For models A and B on the same n emails, write:

  • b = emails where A is correct and B is wrong.
  • c = emails where B is correct and A is wrong.

Every b email adds one success to A relative to B. Every c email subtracts one. Emails where both are correct add zero to the difference; emails where both are wrong also add zero. Therefore:

estimated accuracy difference (A − B) = (b − c) / n

Check yourself

Two models agree on 90 of 100 emails. On the remaining 10, A alone is correct 7 times and B alone is correct 3 times. What is A − B?

Correct: (7 − 3) / 100 = 4 points. Agreement may make each accuracy high or low, but it adds zero to their difference.

The notebook's approximate uncertainty

Give each email a paired difference value: +1 for an A-only success, −1 for a B-only success, and 0 for agreement. Let q = (b + c)/n be the discordance share and d = (b − c)/n be the observed difference. The estimated standard error of the mean paired difference is approximately:

SE(d) ≈ sqrt((q − d²) / n)

When the observed difference is near zero, this becomes sqrt(q / n), and a normal 95% planning half-width is 1.96 × sqrt(q / n). This is the notebook's formula.

This planning approximation assumes independently sampled email units, a stable pairing process, and a normal approximation adequate for the number of discordant pairs. It is especially fragile when b + c is small, when the sample has dependent threads or campaigns, or when a close decision depends on a few discordant emails.

Use a better final interval

For final analysis, resample paired email records together in a paired bootstrap (or resample whole threads if threads remain). For an exact paired accuracy test, use an exact paired method, such as an exact binomial test of the discordant counts / exact McNemar approach. Never bootstrap A and B separately: that breaks the pairing that made the comparison informative.
