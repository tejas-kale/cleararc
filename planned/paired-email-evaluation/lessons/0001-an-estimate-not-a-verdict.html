Lesson 1 of 5 · Paired email evaluation

80 out of 100 is an estimate, not a verdict

Start with a count you can see. Then ask what another reasonable sample of emails could have shown.

≈9 minutes · Single-model accuracy and Wilson intervals

One concrete result

Suppose one model classifies 80 of 100 hand-labelled emails correctly. Its observed accuracy is 80 / 100 = 0.80 = 80%. That is a faithful summary of these 100 emails.

It is not automatically the model's true accuracy for your inbox. A different random draw from the same defined inbox window could contain more awkward newsletters, more ambiguous requests, or simply more of the model's weak cases. The gap between a sample result and the result that repeated samples would produce is sampling uncertainty.

The target here is your defined personal email population, not “all email” or every future inbox. Narrow scope does not eliminate sampling uncertainty; it makes the claim honest.

Check yourself

A second random sample of 100 comparable emails gives 74% accuracy. Which statement is justified?

Correct. A sample proportion moves because which emails happen to be sampled. You need an interval to quantify ordinary movement; averaging two samples would estimate a different, 200-email result.

The ordinary binomial standard error

For a first model, pretend each sampled email is an independent correct/wrong draw with the same underlying probability p. A correct/wrong result has variance p(1 − p). Averaging n such results divides variance by n; taking a square root gives the standard error:

SE(p-hat) = sqrt(p(1 − p) / n)

We do not know the underlying p, so planning commonly substitutes the observed proportion, 0.80. For 80/100:

SE ≈ sqrt(0.80 × 0.20 / 100) = sqrt(0.0016) = 0.040, or about 4 percentage points.

“Standard error” is not an error that happened to the model. It is the typical sampling-scale wobble of the estimated proportion under this binomial approximation.

Why 1.96 appears

With enough independent observations, a sample proportion is approximately bell-shaped around the underlying proportion. About 95% of a standard normal bell lies within 1.96 standard deviations of its centre. That is why the simple normal interval is:

p-hat ± 1.96 × SE

Here that is 0.80 ± 1.96 × 0.040 = 0.80 ± 0.078, or roughly 72.2% to 87.8%. “95%” describes the long-run procedure: if we repeatedly sampled and built intervals this way, about 95% would cover the fixed underlying accuracy. It does not mean a 95% chance that this particular fixed value is in the interval.

Why use Wilson instead?

The normal interval is a useful derivation, but it treats the estimated proportion's standard error as fixed. Near 0% or 100%, or with small samples, it can be too optimistic and can even run below 0 or above 100. The Wilson score interval solves the score-test inequality instead. It has better coverage for proportions and stays in the possible range.

For p-hat = 0.80, n = 100, and z = 1.95996, so z² = 3.84146:

  1. Denominator: 1 + z²/n = 1 + 3.84146/100 = 1.03841.
  2. Centre: (p-hat + z²/(2n)) / denominator = (0.80 + 3.84146/200) / 1.03841 = 0.7889.
  3. Half-width: z × sqrt[p-hat(1 − p-hat)/n + z²/(4n²)] / denominator = 1.95996 × sqrt(0.0016 + 0.0000960) / 1.03841 = 0.0777.
  4. Interval: 0.7889 ± 0.0777 = 71.1% to 86.7%.
Read the result precisely

80/100 is evidence consistent with accuracy around 80%, but the notebook's 95% Wilson interval is 71.1%–86.7%. It supports an exploratory description, not a precise claim that the model is exactly 80% accurate.

Retrieval practice

Why does multiplying a standard error by 1.96 make a 95% interval only approximately, rather than exactly?

The multiplier comes from the standard normal bell. The sample proportion only approaches that bell under assumptions and sufficient sample size; its estimated standard error is also imperfect. Wilson improves the proportion interval's actual coverage, especially at small samples or near boundaries.
